Strategy 12 · Zerodha orders · Fyers data · NIFTY OPTIONS · Plain-English edition

SIMPLE_9:30

Every other strategy in this app reads the NIFTY chart and then buys an option. This one never looks at the NIFTY chart at all. At 09:25 it finds the two option contracts trading closest to ₹180 — one Call, one Put — and then simply waits to see which of them trades above ₹180 first. That one gets bought. One trade, one day.

1. The idea in one minute

An option contract that costs about ₹180 is a particular kind of animal. It is deep enough in-the-money that it moves nearly point-for-point with NIFTY, but cheap enough that ₹180 is a level a real morning move can push through. The whole strategy is built on that one number.

09:25 — quote every in-the-money strike on both sides. Keep the ONE Call and the ONE Put trading nearest ₹180. That pair is the day's watchlist, and it never changes again.

09:25 → 09:35 — watch both premiums. The first one to trade above ₹180 is bought at market, immediately. It might be the Call, it might be the Put. If neither gets there by 09:35, the day is over before it started.

The stop — a flat 20 points below whatever you actually paid. It only starts following the premium once the trade has proved itself by touching the top of the box (₹220); until then the flat stop is the only risk. It only ever moves up.

09:45 — if the trade has spent those minutes shuffling between ₹160 and ₹220 without ever leaving that box, close it. Profit or loss, it does not matter: a move that has not happened in fifteen minutes is not going to.

That is the entire strategy. There is no RSI, no moving average, no VWAP, no VIX gate, no open-interest filter, no confirmation candle. It reads two numbers — the premium of one Call and the premium of one Put — and a clock.

2. Why the premium chart, not the NIFTY chart

Most option strategies decide on the index and then translate that decision into a contract. This one decides on the contract directly, and that is a deliberate difference rather than a shortcut.

A ₹180 in-the-money option is mostly intrinsic value — the part that is simply "how far past the strike is NIFTY". So a 20-point move in the premium is very nearly a 20-point move in NIFTY. But not exactly: the remaining time value expands when the market gets nervous and drains as the day wears on. The premium therefore carries something the index chart cannot show you — what the market is willing to pay for that move right now.

The practical consequence. The two watchlist legs are not mirror images. On a quiet morning NIFTY can drift 30 points up and neither leg reaches ₹180, because the Call's gain is eaten by decay while the Put falls. That is not a bug in the rule — it is the rule refusing a move the option market did not price as a move.

4. Step 1 — 09:25, building the watchlist

At 09:25 the engine takes one reading of NIFTY, rounds it to the ATM strike, and asks Fyers for a quote on every rung of the ladder — the ATM contract and eight strikes in-the-money on each side. Then it keeps exactly one strike per side: the one whose premium is closest to ₹180.

The Paper page shows exactly this table, live, so you can always see what the engine had to choose from — including any rung that came back without a price.

Three details that matter more than they look.
  • It happens once. The watchlist is frozen at 09:25 and never re-picked. A watchlist that drifted with NIFTY would be a different strategy — and could never be reproduced by /replay.
  • A missing quote is reported, never assumed. If a rung comes back empty it is listed as missing rather than treated as ₹0 — which would otherwise win the "closest to ₹180" contest outright by being 180 away in the wrong direction.
  • Ties are broken toward the money. Two strikes exactly the same distance from ₹180 → the one nearer the ATM wins. Cheaper, and more of it moves.

5. Step 2 — the ₹180 break

From the moment the watchlist exists until 09:35, both premiums are polled about once a second. The first one to print above ₹180 is bought at market. Exactly ₹180 is not a break — the level has to be cleared.

In the run above the Call cleared the level at 09:30 at ₹180.90, and the position opened there with a stop at ₹160.90 — twenty points below what was actually paid, not twenty points below ₹180. That distinction matters on a fast morning: if the fill had come at ₹186 the stop would sit at ₹166, so a slipped fill never quietly buys itself a wider stop.

If both legs are above ₹180 on the same reading — which happens on a violently indecisive open — the engine takes the one further through the level. It has travelled more, and an arbitrary "Calls first" rule would bias every such morning to one side.

6. Step 3 — the 20-point stop, and when it starts to follow

The stop starts twenty points under the fill and simply sits there. It does not follow the premium straight away — the trail stays disarmed until the premium actually touches the top of the 09:45 box (₹220 at the defaults, the same bandUp level the sideways exit reads).

This matters. An earlier build armed the trail at entry, so a fill at ₹185 that wobbled up six points ratcheted its stop 17 times and closed the trade for a loss the real ₹165 stop never came near — the trade was stopped out by its own trail. Now a trade that has not reached ₹220 can only leave by the flat stop or the clock.

Once armed, it ratchets: twenty points behind the highest premium seen, up only, never back below where it started. Set SIMPLE930_TRAIL_ARM_AT_BAND_UP=false to restore the old arm-at-entry behaviour.

The trade in section 5 ran to ₹241, so it did arm. Here is the moment it crossed ₹220 and the trail took over:

Everything before ₹220 is protected by the flat ₹160.90 stop alone — the wobble up to ₹191.63 moves nothing. Once armed, the trail ratchets with each new high, and the trade ended when the premium came back to it at 10:18. Peak ₹241, stop ₹221, exit ₹220.31 — +39.41 points.

Why the trail is not optional here. With a 20-point stop and no target, the only two ways out are the stop and the clock. Without a trail, a trade that ran to ₹241 and came back to ₹181 would exit at breakeven after being ₹60 in front. The trail is what turns "the move happened" into money.

7. Step 4 — the 09:45 rule

At 09:45 the engine asks one question about a trade that is still open: has the premium been outside ₹160–₹220 at any point since entry? If yes, nothing happens — the trade has proved it is going somewhere, and the trail owns it from here to the 15:15 square-off. If no, it is closed at market on the spot, whatever the P&L.

That run bought at ₹181.40 at 09:31 and spent the next fourteen minutes between ₹176 and ₹191. It never came near ₹220 and never near ₹160, so at 09:45 it was closed at ₹181.25: fifteen paise below the fill, −₹89.18 after charges. The rule did precisely what it exists to do — it declined to keep paying decay on a trade that was not moving.

The trade in section 5 shows the other branch. It cleared ₹220 at 09:43, two minutes before the deadline, and the 09:45 check simply did not apply to it. It went on to make ₹2,870.

An honest note about the lower edge. The rule says "not above ₹220 or below ₹160". With a 20-point stop the lower edge can never actually be the thing that saves a trade — a fill at ₹181 stops out at ₹161, which is a rupee above the box floor. The ₹160 edge only becomes live if you widen SIMPLE930_SL_PTS past the box offset. It is implemented faithfully all the same.

8. The day it does nothing

This will be the commonest outcome, and it is worth looking at rather than skipping. Both legs were picked normally; neither ever cleared ₹180.

The Call's best print in the window was ₹178.95 and the Put's was ₹176.68. At 09:36 the engine closed the day, wrote the reason to the skip log, and stopped. No trade, no charges, nothing at risk.

The chart deliberately runs on past the window so you can see the part that stings: the Call did clear ₹180 — at 09:38, three minutes after the window shut. That is not a missed signal, it is the rule working. The whole premise is that a move which has not declared itself by 09:35 is a different move; if you find yourself wanting those trades, the setting to change is SIMPLE930_ENTRY_END, and you should change it on evidence rather than on one chart.

Read the skip log, not just the trade log. A run of no-trade days is the signal that ₹180 is the wrong number for the current regime — the ladder may be sitting at ₹240 on a fresh weekly, or at ₹90 on expiry day. The Paper page's decision trail and ~/trading-data/skips/simple930_paper_skips_*.jsonl both record the peak each leg reached, so the question "how close did it get?" always has an answer.

9. The money, with real numbers

One lot of NIFTY is 65 units on this install (NIFTY_LOT_SIZE), so one premium point is ₹65. The round trip on Zerodha, computed by this repo's own charges model on a ₹181 contract, is ₹79.39 — about 1.06 premium points. Add the 1.5 points a side the backtest charges for slippage and the trade has to find roughly 4 points before it is worth anything at all.

The trend day (section 5)₹180.90₹220.31 (trail)+39.41₹2,561.65₹85.04+₹2,476.61
The sideways day (section 7)₹181.40₹181.25 (09:45)−0.15−₹9.75₹79.43−₹89.18
A clean stop-out₹181.00₹161.00 (stop)−20.00−₹1,300.00₹76.51−₹1,376.51

The shape of the thing is therefore: lose ₹1,376.51 when you are wrong, lose about ₹90 when nothing happens, and make whatever the morning gives you when you are right. That third number is the entire edge, and it is the one number nobody has measured yet.

The arithmetic that has to work. With a fixed 20-point risk, this strategy needs its winners to average more than 20 points for every loser it takes. The sideways rule exists to make that possible — it converts most "nothing happened" days from a full 20-point stop into a ₹90 fee. Whether it succeeds is exactly what paper collection has to find out.

10. Safety limits

LimitDefaultWhat it does
SIMPLE930_MAX_DAILY_TRADES1One trade a day. Once it exits, the day is finished — the other leg cannot trigger later.
SIMPLE930_MAX_DAILY_LOSS0 (off)Redundant at one trade a day; raise it only if you raise the trade count.
SIMPLE930_LTP_STALE_MS15000A premium the poll has not refreshed for 15 seconds is not evidence. An entry on a stale quote is refused rather than filled.
SIMPLE930_FORCED_EXIT15:15Hard square-off. Nothing is carried overnight, ever.
Triple live gateall offA real order needs SIMPLE930_LIVE_ENABLED=true AND LIVE_HARNESS_DRY_RUN=false AND SIMPLE930_LIVE_DRY_RUN not true.

Beyond that, the entry is refused outright — no position is opened at all — if the stop cannot be computed, if the premium falls back to or below ₹180 between the trigger and the fill, or if the expiry cannot be resolved. A trade with no stop is worse than a missed trade.

11. Paper, Live, Backtest & Replay

ModeWhereWhat it does
Paper/simple930-paper/statusThe canonical engine. Live Fyers premiums, simulated orders. Shows the 09:25 ladder, both watchlist legs, both premium charts and a decision trail of every choice made.
Live/simple930-liveThe same paper code with a harness attached that turns its entry and exit into real Zerodha orders. Live cannot disagree with paper, because it is paper.
Backtest/simple930-backtestReal 1-minute option premium candles from Fyers — no delta model, no theta model. See the caveat below.
Replay/replayRe-runs a recorded session tick by tick against the paper engine. Every premium the strategy reads is written to the tick archive, including the whole 09:25 ladder, so the watchlist itself is reproducible.
The backtest can only reach back as far as the listed contracts. A NIFTY weekly option is delisted the moment it expires and Fyers then refuses the symbol outright, so last month's premiums cannot be fetched at any price. In practice that means the current expiry week. The results page says so per session — an unfetchable day is reported as "no option data", never quietly counted as a day with no trade.

Two small, deliberate differences between the backtest and paper, both documented in the backtest's own header: paper fills at the first premium it observes above ₹180 (₹180.90 in the run above), while the backtest fills a 1-minute bar that crosses the level at the level itself and then charges slippage on top; and paper checks its exits about once a second, while the backtest checks once a bar. Expect fills to differ by a point or two in either direction.

13. What's still missing

  • It has never traded. Zero paper sessions, zero live orders. Every number in this guide comes from the engine reading constructed candles, not from a market.
  • ₹180 has not been validated against anything. It is the operator's number, not a fitted one. Nobody has measured how often a ₹180 ITM contract clears ₹180 in the first ten minutes, nor what it does afterwards.
  • Nor has 09:45. The fifteen-minute box is a judgement about how quickly a real opening move declares itself. It might be cutting winners short; it might not be cutting losers fast enough. There is no data either way.
  • Backtest history is measured in days, not years. Weekly options are delisted at expiry, so the usable range is roughly the current expiry week. Whatever this strategy is judged on will mostly have to be forward-collected paper sessions.
  • The Fyers token was expired when this was built. No real premium series could be pulled, so the guide's candles are constructed. The decisions on them are real; the prices are not.
  • The strike search assumes the ladder reaches ₹180. On a fresh weekly the whole ITM ladder can sit above the trigger, and the engine will then watch the closest thing it has — possibly a ₹260 contract for which "breaking ₹180" is meaningless. SIMPLE930_MAX_PREMIUM_DIST exists for exactly that day and ships OFF, because the rule as written does not mention it.
  • Exits are checked about once a second, not tick by tick. The backtest and replay check them once per bar. Expect fills to differ.
