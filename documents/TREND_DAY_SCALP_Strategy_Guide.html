Trend Day Scalp

Most days NIFTY goes nowhere in particular, and a bought option quietly bleeds while it does. This strategy spends the first hour deciding whether today is worth trading at all. On most days the answer is no, and it does nothing. When the answer is yes, it buys one dip in the direction the day already chose, risks a fixed 12–18 points, and takes a fixed profit.

Updated 2026-08-09  ·  plain-English edition  ·  every number below is produced by running the live engine

Read this first: this strategy has never traded. Not one paper session, not one live order. Every figure in this guide comes from the engine running on constructed candles. There is no win rate here because none has been measured.

1. The idea in one minute

Buying an option is a race against the clock. Every day you hold one, time decay takes a slice, so a day where price wanders sideways is a slow loss even when you are not wrong. The usual response is to trade more carefully. This strategy's response is to trade far less often.

At 10:15 it looks at the first hour once and asks three questions. Did the market actually move? Has it stayed on one side of its average price? Has it committed, or is it hugging the middle? If any answer is no, the day is over before it began — no trade, no loss, nothing.

When all three are yes, the direction is locked for the rest of the day and it waits for one thing: a dip back to the average price that gets bought straight back up. That is the entry. From there the trade has a fixed stop, a fixed target, and one single move of the stop to break-even. Nothing trails, nothing is left to judgement.

2. Why it was built this way

The other seven strategies in this app share a shape: they buy a breakout and let the winner run. That produces a handful of very large winners carrying a long tail of small losses. ORB's own notes record nine trades where the single best one was 211% of the total profit — remove that one trade and the strategy loses money.

This one is built to be the opposite: low but steady. It gives up the huge winner on purpose, in exchange for a result that does not depend on catching it.

The number that shaped every rule below.

On one lot, a round trip costs about ₹88 in statutory charges plus roughly 1.5 points of slippage on each side (~₹195). Call it ₹300 gone before the trade is right about anything. At a delta of 0.6, that is about 17 index points of movement just to break even.

That is why the target is 2.5× the risk and not a scalper's ten points. A strategy whose average win is twelve points cannot survive its own costs, no matter how often it is right.

A word on "points". Every price on these charts is the index level itself — not rupees, and not the price of the option you buy. So "+30 points" means the index moved 30 points our way. As a rough rule of thumb the option we buy gains a little over half a point for each point the index moves, and one lot is about 75 units — so a 30-point index move is roughly ₹1,200 on one lot before costs.

All three passed, so this became a CE-only day: from 10:15 until the close it will consider buying calls and nothing else. It will not flip if price reverses. It will not take a put. The direction was decided once.

Why 0.20 — and why the first two answers were wrong. This threshold was originally set by arithmetic on an idealised path: on a day that climbs in a perfectly straight line the average price sits exactly halfway between the low and the high, so spot finishes 0.5× the range above VWAP. That reasoning gave 0.6 first, then 0.35. Both were wrong, because real first hours do not climb in straight lines — VWAP tracks price much more closely than the idealised case. Measured over 39 real sessions the distance is median 0.18× the range and p90 is 0.39. A 0.35 setting therefore sat near the 85th percentile and passed just 13% of days, which is how the first backtest of this strategy produced five trades in a year. 0.20 sits just above the measured median: roughly the more-committed half of days. It is not the value that scored best on that sample — 0.15 did — because 17 trades cannot tell those two apart, and picking the winner of a noisy sweep is how you fit a curve to nothing.

Four things have to line up on one closed candle:

  1. Price dipped to the zone. Within the last 3 candles, the low reached whichever of VWAP / EMA20 is nearer. A wick counts — on a real trend day price often just grazes the line and bounces.
  2. It closed back through it. The dip was bought, not continued.
  3. The move is fresh. Either this candle itself dipped in, or the one before closed on the wrong side. This stops it buying the fifth candle of a rally that left the zone long ago.
  4. The candle means it. Green, with a body of at least 0.4× ATR. A tiny indecisive candle is not a reclaim.

On the day above that happened at 11:20. The zone sat at 24337.91 (the EMA20, which was nearer than VWAP at 24319.73). Price dipped to 24335.41 and closed back at 24353.41 with a body of 14 points — comfortably past the 7.26 points required by that day's ATR of 18.14.

6. Step 3 — How we get out

Everything is decided at entry. There is no judgement left afterwards.

LevelWhereWhy
Stop24335.41 — 18 points below entryThe low of the dip. If the dip that was supposed to be bought keeps going, the read was wrong.
Target24398.41 — 2.5R, 45.0 pointsTaken and done. Not trailed past, not held for more.

On this day the break-even jump armed at 11:25 and the target was reached at 11:55 — Target hit (2.5R) at 24398.41.

The same machine pointing the other way. The grey band on the left is the first hour, and at 10:15 it is judged once: the day moved 0.63% (needs 0.5%), the last six closes were all below VWAP, and price sat 74 points from VWAP against the 31 required. Three passes, so this is a PE-only day — from here it will not buy a single Call no matter what price does. Later the market bounces back up into the zone (the nearer of the purple VWAP and blue EMA20 lines), and one decisive red candle rejects it and closes back below → BUY PE at 24,483. The stop goes at the top of that bounce, 24 points away, and that distance is our "1R". Target is fixed at 2.5R = 60 points. Ten minutes later the trade is up 1R so the stop makes its one and only move, up to break-even. Half an hour after that the target prints and we are done — no trailing, no second-guessing.

And the loser, which looks almost identical until it doesn't. Good day gate, clean dip into the zone, a convincing green candle reclaiming it → BUY CE at 24,443, stop 21 points below at the low of the dip. Then price hangs about for a few candles, never gets the 1R it needs to move the stop to break-even, and rolls over. 25 minutes after entry the dip low gives way and we are out for exactly 1R. That is the whole design in one picture: every loss is the same size, and every win is 2.5 of them. You do not need to be right often — at this shape, being right 29% of the time is break-even.

Why there is no trailing stop. Every other strategy in this app trails, and it is exactly what turns their winners into scratches — a normal pullback taps the trail and the trade is closed just before the move continues. Here the fixed target does that job instead. The stop makes precisely one move, to break-even, and then holds still.

7. Days it says no

This is the part that matters most, because it is what most days look like.

Price here keeps crossing its own average. The gate reports:

TestResult
first-hour range❌ 30pt = 0.12% of 24180 (need >= 0.5%)
6 closes one side of VWAP❌ crosses VWAP 24191 within the last 6 bars
extension from VWAP✅ 11pt vs need 6pt (0.20 x range)

Two failures, so the day is off. Note the third test passed — that is the point of having three. Price was 11 points from VWAP, which clears the bar, but on a range of only 30 points that is meaningless. A single test would have been fooled.

A day that trades nothing produces ₹0. On a strategy built around buying options, ₹0 on a bad day is the whole edge.

These exclude slippage, which in practice takes roughly another ₹200 off every round trip. Add that back before judging any of it.

Do the arithmetic yourself before believing any of this. With a win of about ₹1667 and a loss of about ₹785, and slippage on top, you need to win roughly four trades in ten just to stand still. Whether this strategy does that is completely unknown. Nothing here is evidence that it does.

10. Paper, Live, Backtest & Replay

WhereWhat it is
PaperThe real engine on live prices, simulated fills. This is the authority — the other two copy it.
BacktestSame signal engine over a date range. Where a candle touched both the stop and the target, it books the loss; a candle that gapped past a level fills at the open, never the better price.
LiveWraps Paper, so live decisions cannot drift from paper ones. Real orders need three switches on at once and are off by default.
ReplayRe-runs a recorded day tick by tick. This is how you check that live and paper agree.

12. What's still missing

Nothing here has been validated. Zero paper sessions, zero live trades. Treat all of it as a starting guess. Two of the constants have already been caught being wrong. The stop cap shipped at 18 points and the extension gate at 0.35, both derived from arithmetic rather than from candles — together they produced five trades in the whole of 2025 and none in 2026. Measured against real sessions, valid setups need a median stop of 35 points, so the 18pt cap was discarding 48 of every 53 setups and keeping only the shallowest. They are now 40 and 0.20, set from the measured distribution. On a 39-session sample that turns 0 trades into 10 — but the net on those 10 is −₹738. The strategy trades now; it is not yet shown to make money, and nothing in this guide should be read as saying otherwise.

Before this is trusted with money:

  1. Run Paper for a long stretch. Because it stands aside on most days, a couple of weeks will produce only a handful of trades — that is not a sample.
  2. Replay those recorded days and confirm the replayed decisions match what Paper did.
  3. Only then look at the live switches, and leave LIVE_HARNESS_DRY_RUN=true for a while first, reading the "order I would have placed" log lines.
Known limitations, stated plainly.
  • Backtest rupees are simulated. There is no historical option-price data, so premiums are modelled from the index move plus time decay. Direction is meaningful; the exact ₹ is not.
  • The premium stop cannot be backtested honestly. It fires on the modelled premium curve, not on a real volatility collapse.
  • Very few trades. The day gate is designed to reject most sessions, so any statistic from a short range is noise. On the measured sample the gate passes about 31% of days and a trade follows on roughly a quarter of those — about 10 trades per 39 sessions.
  • No volatility or open-interest awareness. A chaotic day is treated exactly like a calm one.
  • The three gate thresholds have never been tested against each other. They may be redundant, or one may be doing all the work. Only a long paper sample will tell.
  • 1 lot only. There is no position sizing beyond the lot multiplier, and no averaging.
