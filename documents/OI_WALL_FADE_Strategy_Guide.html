OI Wall Fade

Every other strategy in this app is a trend or a breakout strategy, so all of them struggle on the same kind of day: the one where price goes nowhere. This one is built for exactly that day, and it is the only engine here that reads something other than price — it reads where the option writers have put their money.

Updated 2026-08-13  ·  plain-English edition  ·  every number below is produced by running the live engine

Read this first: this strategy has never traded, and it can never be backtested. Not one paper session, not one live order — and unlike the other nine strategies there is no simulated history either, because Fyers publishes no historical per-strike Open Interest. There is nothing to run a backtest against and there never will be. The only evidence that will ever exist about this idea is forward-recorded paper sessions. Every chart below is the real engine run on constructed candles and a constructed OI ladder, for illustration.

1. The idea in one minute

Somebody has to be on the other side of every option you buy. The people who sell options — the writers — make money when price stays away from the strike they sold. So when a huge number of Calls have been sold at, say, 24800, the people who sold them have a very direct financial interest in NIFTY not getting there. That strike becomes a ceiling, and traders call it a wall.

Open Interest is simply the count of contracts still open at each strike. The strike with the most open Calls is the ceiling; the strike with the most open Puts is the floor. Between them is a range, and on a quiet day price tends to stay inside it.

Find the two walls→ Price presses one of them→ Is that wall's OI still growing?→ Yes → the candle must close back away→ Fade it towards the middle

The crucial part is the third box. A wall only means something while the writers are still there. If the OI at that strike is falling while price leans on it, the writers are buying their positions back — closing up shop — and the wall is about to give way. That is the exact moment a range trade turns into a trend loss, so the engine reads it as a stand aside signal rather than a weaker version of the same trade.

2. Why Open Interest, and not another indicator

Every indicator this app already has is made out of price. EMA, Bollinger Bands, VWAP, SuperTrend, ATR, RSI — all of them are arithmetic performed on the same candles. Averaging a number in a new way does not create new information about the market; it only smooths what you already had. That is why adding a tenth momentum strategy would mostly have added correlated risk.

Open Interest is genuinely different. It is a count of open positions, published by the exchange, and it says something price cannot: how many people are committed at a given level, and whether that commitment is growing or shrinking right now.

The data was already flowing through this app and being thrown away. optionChainRecorder polls the ATM±N Call and Put chain every few seconds for prices, and the exchange sends the OI field in those very same rows. It is now kept, fed into services/oiChain.js, and that ladder is what this engine reads. No extra API calls were added.

3. Words you'll see

  • Open Interest (OI) — how many contracts are currently open at one strike. Not volume: volume counts trades, OI counts positions still standing.
  • Wall — the strike with the most open contracts. The highest-OI Call strike is the ceiling; the highest-OI Put strike is the floor.
  • The band — the space between the two walls. Its middle is the target.
  • Defended — the wall's OI is still rising: more contracts are being written there. The writers are holding the line.
  • Shedding — the wall's OI is falling: writers are buying back and leaving. The wall is failing.
  • ΔOI ("delta OI") — the percentage change in a strike's OI. Measured over the last few real changes, not over clock time (see below).
  • Fading — betting that price turns back from a level rather than breaking through it.
"Three OI moves" is not "three minutes". The chain is polled every few seconds, but the exchange only republishes OI when it actually changes. The engine therefore stores a new sample only when the number moves, and OIWF_OI_LOOKBACK counts those real moves. That makes a 4% change mean the same thing on a busy morning and a dead afternoon. The price of that choice is that the lookback covers a variable amount of clock time — which is why OIWF_MAX_OI_SPAN_SEC exists, and why a reading that took more than 30 minutes to accumulate is thrown away rather than traded.

4. Step 1 — Finding the two walls

Every few seconds the recorder writes the OI of every strike around the money. The engine takes the highest-OI Call strike and the highest-OI Put strike and calls them the band. In the example below the band is 24500 to 24800 — 300 points wide.

Why the band has to be at least 150 points. The target is the middle of the band, so a 300-point band only offers about 150 points of room and a 150-point band offers about 75. Below that, the option spread plus the theta burnt while waiting eats the whole move. This is not a threshold that tuning can rescue — if the walls are close together there is simply no trade there, whatever the OI says.

A word on "points". Every price on these charts is the NIFTY 50 index level itself — not rupees, and not the price of the option you buy. As a rough rule of thumb the slightly in-the-money option this strategy buys gains a little over half a point for each point the index moves, and one lot is 65 units — so a 100-point index move is roughly ₹3,900 on one lot before costs.

5. Step 2 — Is the wall being defended?

Price reaching a wall is not the signal. The signal is price reaching a wall that is still being reinforced. The engine looks at that one strike's own OI over the last OIWF_OI_LOOKBACK changes (default 3) and asks which of three things is happening:

What the wall's OI is doingWhat it meansWhat the engine does
rising ≥ +2%Writers are adding. They are defending the level.DEFEND — this is the trade
between −2% and +2%Nothing is happening. Nobody is defending anything.No trade
falling ≤ −2%Writers are buying back and leaving. The wall is failing.BREAK — stand aside
Unknown is a refusal, never a zero. If the ladder cannot supply a ΔOI for that strike — too few samples yet, the strike has drifted out of the polled window, the recorder stalled — the engine skips the trade and says so. Treating "I don't know" as "0%" would quietly turn every unknown into a "nobody is defending" reading, and the two want completely different actions.

6. Step 3 — The candle has to reject

The last thing required is price confirmation: the 5-minute candle that touched the wall must close back on the safe side of it — red and below a Call wall, green and above a Put wall. A candle that pokes the wall and closes above it has not rejected anything; it is mid-breakout.

This is also what keeps the decision reproducible. The candle is a closed bar from the Fyers history endpoint, so it is exactly the same number on every re-run.

The trade that works. The band is 24500–24800, so the middle sits at 24650. Price runs up into the 24800 Call wall and pokes through to 24805 — but that strike's OI is still rising, +4.2% over its last 3 changes, accumulated in 300 seconds. The writers have not budged. The candle closes red at 24765, back below the wall, and the engine buys a Put. Target 24650 (115 points away), stop 24825 — 60 points away, sitting 25 points on the far side of the wall.

The engine's own description of that entry, verbatim:

OI WALL FADE PE: the CE wall at 24800 (OI 912400) is DEFENDED — ΔOI +4.2% over 3 move(s) in 300s, and the candle rejected off it (24805/24759 → close 24765) | band 24500–24800 (300pt) | target = mid-band 24650 (115pt) | SL = 25pt beyond the wall at 24825 (60pt) | R:R 1.92

7. Step 4 — How we get out

  1. The middle of the band — price reaches 24650. That is the whole trade. Take it.
  2. The wall breaks — price pushes 25 points past the strike we were fading. The thesis was that the writers would hold, and they did not.
  3. The clock — square off at OIWF_FORCED_EXIT (default 15:15).

Nothing trails, and — importantly — nothing exits on OI. There is no trailing stop, no break-even jump, no partial booking and no time stop. And if the wall starts shedding OI while we are in the trade, that does not close the position: the exit is the price stop and only the price stop. The wall's OI at the moment of exit is written onto the trade record so the question "was the wall already failing when we lost?" can be answered later from data — but it does not act.

8. The geometry that decides your reward

Both levels are fixed by the walls: the target is the middle of the band, the stop is 25 points past the wall. The distance between them never changes. What does change is where the rejection candle happens to close — and that alone decides how the fixed distance is split between reward and risk.

Read that table the right way round. A candle that only just turns away from the wall gives the best reward-to-risk, because the stop is close behind it. A dramatic-looking plunge back into the middle of the band is a worse trade: you have already spent much of the move you were trying to capture, and the stop is now a long way off. The last row is a real trade the engine will take at 0.4 reward-to-risk, because the rules as written do not forbid it. OIWF_MIN_TARGET_PTS is the lever for that, and it ships switched off rather than baking a guess into the default.

9. The trades it refuses

The same candle, and no trade at all. Identical price action — price presses the 24800 wall and closes back below it. The only difference is in the ladder: this time the wall's OI is falling 6.4%. The writers are buying their Calls back and getting out of the way, which is precisely what happens just before a level gives. Fading here is how a range strategy takes a trending loss. The engine's own words: DO NOT FADE — price is at the CE wall 24800 and its OI is FALLING -6.4% (≤ -2%): the writers are running and the wall is giving way

A properly defended wall we still refuse. The 24700 Call wall is building at +5.0%, the candle rejects cleanly — everything about the setup is right except the room. The Put wall is at 24600, so the band is only 100 points and the middle is a mere 50 points away. Fifty points on a slightly in-the-money option is roughly ₹1,950 gross on one lot, and the spread plus the theta burnt waiting eats a large slice of that. The engine says so plainly: Wall band is only 100pt (24600–24700) < 150pt — too narrow for a fade to pay for the round trip

And here is the same good setup going wrong:

The trade that loses. Same entry at 24765, same defended wall — and then price simply goes through it anyway. The stop is 60 points away at 24825, and it is hit. This is the trade the BREAK rule is trying to filter out and sometimes will not: OI is published with a lag, so writers can start running before the number says they have.

Other reasons it will stand aside on a given day: the ladder never produced both a Call wall and a Put wall (the recorder was off, or the token expired); price spent the day in the middle and never came within 30 points of either wall; the candle closed outside the band entirely, so there is no range left to fade; or the day's trade budget was already spent.

The number that matters: 35.52%. At that reward-to-risk this strategy has to be right about 35.52% of the time just to finish flat after costs. Nobody has measured whether it is, and nobody can measure it from history. That figure also moves trade by trade, because reward-to-risk swings with where the rejection candle closes — the 0.4 row in section 8 needs roughly a 70% win rate to break even on its own.

11. Safety limits

  • Max 3 trades a day (OIWF_MAX_DAILY_TRADES).
  • Day ends after 2 stop-outs (OIWF_MAX_DAILY_LOSSES) — and here a stop-out means a wall actually broke, which is itself evidence the day is trending rather than ranging. Two of those and the strategy's whole premise is wrong for the session.
  • Day ends at ₹3,000 of loss (OIWF_MAX_DAILY_LOSS).
  • Entries only between 09:45 and 14:45, everything squared off by 15:15. The late start is not caution — the ΔOI test needs several real OI changes to have happened first.
  • Position size follows the global setting — OIWF_LOT_MULTIPLIER defaults to 0, meaning "use LOT_MULTIPLIER", and the app-wide MAX_LOT_MULTIPLIER ceiling still applies.
  • The shared portfolio cap and the weekly loss cap both apply on top.
It refuses to start without an OI feed. If OPTION_CHAIN_RECORDER_ENABLED or OPTION_CHAIN_RECORD_OI is off, the ladder stays empty and this strategy has no input whatsoever. Rather than sitting quietly all day looking healthy, /oi-wall-fade-paper/start refuses outright and names the setting to turn on.

12. Paper, Live & Replay

ModeRouteWhat it does
Paper/oi-wall-fade-paperThe canonical engine. Real data, simulated orders. Every rule lives here.
Live/oi-wall-fade-liveWraps Paper, so Live and Paper cannot disagree. Triple-gated to dry-run.
Replay/replayRe-runs a recorded session — with a large caveat, below.
Backtestnone, and there never will beFyers publishes no historical per-strike OI. There is nothing to simulate against.
Replay reproduces the candles but NOT the walls. The price half of every decision comes from the Fyers history endpoint, so it replays exactly. The OI half comes from a live in-memory ladder, and chain_oi.jsonl — which the recorder does write — has no replay timeline yet. On a replay every wall reading therefore comes back UNKNOWN, and the engine refuses rather than fading blind. That is the safe failure, not a silent one, but it does mean a replay of this strategy cannot currently verify its decisions. Because of that, the full OI context of every decision is written onto the trade record and the skip log instead: which wall, its OI, its ΔOI, how long that Δ took, both walls, the band and the band PCR.
How the data actually arrives. Closed 5-minute NIFTY 50 candles come from the Fyers history endpoint, refreshed once per bar a few seconds after it closes. The live index price that the two exit levels are tested against comes from the app's shared tick socket — so exits are checked tick by tick, not on a poll. The option premium is polled separately every OIWF_OPT_POLL_MS, but that is for P&L display only; no exit rule reads it.

14. What's still missing

  • It has never traded. Zero paper sessions, zero live orders. Every number in this guide comes from the engine run on constructed candles and a constructed ladder, not from a market.
  • There is no backtest, and there cannot be one. No historical per-strike OI exists to run against. Every other strategy here can be judged on years of simulated history before it risks a rupee; this one can only be judged on paper sessions collected day by day, going forward.
  • Replay cannot verify it either. Until chain_oi.jsonl gets a replay timeline, a replay reproduces the candles and refuses every wall reading. The usual "diff Paper against Replay before going live" check is therefore not available for this strategy — which makes the bar for touching the live gate higher here, not lower.
  • The core claim is untested. Does a wall whose OI is rising actually hold price? Nobody knows. That is the single question the read-only /oi-monitor page and these paper sessions exist to answer, and it may well turn out to be no.
  • Every threshold is a round number. 150-point band, 30-point proximity, ±2% ΔOI, 25-point stop, 3-move lookback — none of these were fitted to anything, because there is nothing to fit them to. Treat them as starting points, not as findings.
  • The walls are only visible inside the polled window. The recorder covers ATM±N strikes (5 by default, so ±250 points). If the true highest-OI strike sits outside that window, the engine reports the edge strike as the wall and flags atBandEdge on the trade. OIWF_REQUIRE_INNER_WALL turns that flag into a refusal; it ships off.
  • OI is published with a lag. Writers can start unwinding before the number moves, so the BREAK filter will always be a little late. That is a floor on how well this can ever work, not a bug to be tuned away.
  • A day recorded with the OI capture off is gone for good. There is no way to reconstruct it later.
